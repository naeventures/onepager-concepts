The company

Aldmarsh buys established businesses and holds them. It takes minority and majority positions, it invests its own balance sheet, and it keeps what it buys for as long as the business is better off held.

There is no fund behind the company and no outside investor to report to, which removes the exit calendar that shapes most private capital. That changes the conversation a management team can have: it can be about the next decade rather than the next reporting period. Involvement is by invitation. Where a board wants Aldmarsh in the room it is in the room, and where it does not, the company reads the accounts and stays out of the way.

Aldmarsh does not run auctions, does not buy in order to install a new management team, and does not buy a business it cannot explain to itself in a paragraph. It is not a lender, not an adviser and not a broker. Where a situation needs speed ahead of judgement, it belongs to somebody else.

Founder

Frances Aldmarsh founded Aldmarsh Holdings to do one thing properly: hold a small number of businesses for a long time, and be useful to the people running them.

She spent the earlier part of her career on the operating side rather than the investing side, and that is still the difference in how she works. She has sat in the chair the quarterly call lands in. So she is careful about the distance between a question that helps a management team and a question that only reassures the person asking it, and she asks fewer of the second kind than she used to.

Her view is that most private capital is more impatient than it admits, and that the impatience is structural rather than personal. Aldmarsh is built slower on purpose, and the structure is the proof rather than the promise: no fund, no outside investors, no date by which anything has to be sold.

She reads the accounts herself. She answers her own email, and the address on this page reaches her and nobody else.
